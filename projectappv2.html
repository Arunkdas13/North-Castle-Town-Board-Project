Capture the CO₂, or let it go?

A gas-powered data center makes CO₂. This tool tells you whether catching and burying it beats venting it — for your exact setup.
New here? The whole idea in 60 seconds

Why does a data center make CO₂? New AI data centers often can't wait years for a grid hookup, so they generate their own power on site by burning natural gas. Burning gas makes electricity — and CO₂ (the chemistry: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O).

The choice. Either vent the CO₂ up the stack (and pay a carbon tax, if there is one), or capture it: scrub it out of the exhaust with a chemical sponge, compress it, pipe it away, and inject it a mile underground — which can earn you carbon credits ($85/tonne in the US).

The race. Do the credits you earn (plus taxes you avoid) beat the cost of all that equipment? Big plants win far more easily than small ones. Set the sliders to your situation and watch the verdict below.

Reference shelf

Plain-English glossary
Behind the meter
Power made on site for your own use, skipping the public grid.
PUE
Total facility power ÷ computer power. 1.20 = 20% overhead, mostly cooling.
Capture fraction
The share of exhaust CO₂ the scrubber removes. The rest still escapes.
Dense phase
CO₂ squeezed until it behaves like a liquid — the cheapest form to pipe.
MEA
Monoethanolamine — the standard chemical "sponge" for CO₂, and the basis of all costs here.
45Q
The US tax credit for buried CO₂: $85/tonne, minimum 18,750 tonnes/year for power plants.
Breakeven credit price
The credit price at which capturing costs exactly the same as venting.
GWP
Global Warming Potential — how a gas compares to CO₂, tonne for tonne, over 100 years.
Flaring
Burning methane off at a stack without using the energy. Wasteful, but ~10× better than letting it leak.
TRL
Technology Readiness Level: 1 = idea on paper, 9 = proven in commercial service.
R²
A fit score: 1.0 = the curve passes through every data point.
NETL
National Energy Technology Laboratory — the US Department of Energy lab whose cost baselines are the standard reference for power-plant economics. Source of the v3 data library.
NGCC
Natural gas combined cycle: a gas turbine plus a steam turbine running off its exhaust heat.
CANSOLV
Shell's commercial amine capture system — the vendor-quoted technology behind NETL's capture costs.
EGR
Exhaust gas recycle — routing some exhaust back to the turbine inlet to concentrate CO₂ for cheaper capture.
Capture technologies ranked by maturity (from the report)
TechnologyMaturityOne-sentence version
Amine scrubbing (MEA)TRL 9A liquid sponge soaks CO₂ out of exhaust, releases it when heated — the proven, bankable choice this model is built on.
Physical solventsTRL 9Dissolve CO₂ under pressure — great for concentrated streams, weak for dilute turbine exhaust.
Pressure-swing adsorptionTRL 9Solids grab CO₂ under pressure, let go when it drops — commercial at hydrogen plants.
Cryogenic distillationTRL 9Chill the gas until CO₂ drops out as liquid — proven but energy-hungry.
MembranesTRL 7–9Films that let CO₂ through and block the rest — commercial for gas processing, still maturing for exhaust.
Enzyme-catalysed captureTRL 7–8A biological catalyst speeds up gentler solvents — demo plant running in Quebec.
Next-gen solventsTRL 6–7Designer chemistries that cut the biggest cost: the heat to wring CO₂ back out of the sponge.
Calcium loopingTRL 6–8Lime grabs CO₂ hot and gives it back in a second reactor — a 0.5 Mtpa Texas plant is under construction.
Allam-Fetvedt cycleTRL 6–7A gas power plant redesigned so capture is built in — demonstrated at 50 MW in Texas. The one to watch for new data centers.
Lab-stage ideasTRL 2–5Ionic liquids, encapsulated solvents, electrochemical routes — watch, don't budget.
Honest caveats — read before quoting any number
  • Capture costs are optimistic for gas plants. The source curve was modeled on coal exhaust (~14% CO₂). Gas-turbine exhaust is only 3–4% CO₂, which is harder to scrub — real costs run higher than shown.
  • The report's own words: its figures are "an insight to trends rather than absolute cost values applicable to a project." Early-stage estimates can be off by −50%/+100%.
  • Small plants are extrapolated. The source's smallest studied CO₂ flow is 328,500 t/yr; below that (roughly <100 MW), compression costs are extended beyond the data — a warning appears when this happens.
  • Cooling numbers and storage cost are placeholders pending primary sources; emission factors and 45Q rules come from outside the report.
  • Pipeline costs assume flat, onshore terrain and exclude land. Chart-derived values carry small digitization error; table values are exact.
  • Gas that leaks before reaching the turbine isn't priced in — real supply chains leak 1–3%, adding climate impact neither route pays for here.
  • The v3 data library is display-only. NETL tables use different dollar vintages (2018$) and assumptions (85% capacity factor) than the GCCSI-based model; they are not mixed into any calculation.
